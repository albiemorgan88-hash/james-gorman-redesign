# Selling a House with Solar Panels in Northern Ireland

Solar panels can be a useful feature when selling a home, but buyers need more than the promise of lower electricity use. They will want to understand who owns the system, how it was installed, what paperwork exists and whether any agreement must transfer with the property.

Preparing those answers before marketing can make the feature easier to explain and reduce avoidable questions later in the sale.

## Confirm who owns the panels

Start with the installation contract and invoice. Establish whether the panels are owned outright, financed, leased or linked to another agreement. Do not describe the system as included without restriction until your solicitor has checked the documents.

If a third party has rights connected with the roof, equipment or payments, give the full paperwork to your solicitor at the beginning. They can advise what a buyer and lender may need and whether any consent or transfer process applies.

Keep the position simple in the property marketing. A clear, accurate description is more useful than a broad claim about savings or income that may not apply to the next owner.

## Gather the installation records

Collect the quotation, invoice, commissioning information, warranties, maintenance history and details of the installer. Include any Microgeneration Certification Scheme documentation, electrical certificates and information for the inverter, battery or monitoring equipment.

Record the installation date and identify which parts of the system have been replaced. If paperwork is missing, contact the original installer or warranty provider where possible. Do not recreate or guess at technical details.

Buyers may also ask whether the installation affected the roof or required other approvals. Put relevant drawings, permissions and guarantees with the rest of the property file so your solicitor and surveyor can review them.

## Check the roof and visible equipment

Panels do not remove the need to consider the roof beneath them. If there are known leaks, damaged tiles or unresolved concerns, take appropriate professional advice before photographs and viewings. A buyer's surveyor may inspect the roof condition and the way the system has been installed.

Make sure the inverter, isolators and monitoring equipment are accessible and accurately identified. Do not open electrical equipment or attempt technical work yourself. Use a suitably qualified contractor if the system needs inspection or repair.

If the panels are working, keep recent monitoring information available as context. Present it as a record of the existing system, not a guarantee of future performance or savings.

## Explain any export arrangement carefully

If electricity is exported to the network, gather the supplier or agent correspondence, meter details and recent statements. The Utility Regulator explains that small-scale generators can export unused electricity, but the commercial arrangement still needs to be checked for the individual installation.

Ask your solicitor and provider what happens when ownership changes. Payment terms, account details and eligibility can depend on the agreement, so avoid promising that a particular benefit will transfer automatically.

The [Utility Regulator's micro-generation information](https://www.uregni.gov.uk/news-centre/decision-generation-and-export-electricity-by-micro-generators-published) provides useful background. Questions about your contract should go to the provider and your solicitor.

## Make sure the EPC and listing are current

An Energy Performance Certificate is required before a property is marketed for sale in Northern Ireland. The energy assessor records features of the building and its services, so tell the assessor about the solar installation and provide accurate information.

The Department of Finance explains the seller's responsibilities in its [Northern Ireland EPC guidance](https://www.finance-ni.gov.uk/articles/energy-performance-certificates). Your estate agent will need the relevant energy performance indicator for the marketing.

If substantial work has taken place since an older certificate was produced, ask an accredited assessor whether a new assessment is appropriate. Do not edit or interpret the rating yourself.
