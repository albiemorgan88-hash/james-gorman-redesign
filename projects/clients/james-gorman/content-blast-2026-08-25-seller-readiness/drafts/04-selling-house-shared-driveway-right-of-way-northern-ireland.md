# Selling a House with a Shared Driveway in Northern Ireland

A shared driveway or right of way is not automatically a problem, but buyers need to understand how access works. The legal rights, physical layout and day-to-day arrangements should all be checked before the property is marketed.

Do not rely on a coloured line remembered from an old conversation. Give the title documents and the facts on the ground to the professionals handling the sale.

## Ask your solicitor to check the title

Send your solicitor the Land Registry folio and title plan, deeds or other property records you hold. Point out the driveway, gate, parking spaces and any route used by neighbours or third parties.

The [nidirect property and land information guide](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/how-find-property-and-land-information) explains that title records can include rights of way, covenants and mortgages. It also notes that a title plan does not usually show ownership of individual boundary features.

Your solicitor can explain the rights and restrictions that apply to the property. An estate agent should not be asked to interpret the legal title or promise access rights that have not been confirmed.

## Separate legal rights from everyday habits

Neighbours may have used an area in a particular way for years, but an informal habit is not the same as the written legal position. Tell your solicitor how the access and parking work in practice and whether that has ever been questioned.

Do not advertise an area as private parking if it is shared access or if the title does not support exclusive use. Clear wording helps buyers understand the arrangement before they become committed to the property.

If a gate, key, barrier or shared surface is involved, explain how it currently operates. Avoid saying that an arrangement will continue unchanged unless the relevant rights and responsibilities have been checked.

## Gather maintenance information

Buyers may ask who repairs the surface, clears drainage, maintains gates or contributes to costs. Collect written agreements, invoices and correspondence that show what has happened previously.

If there is no formal maintenance arrangement, say so to your solicitor rather than creating one for the purpose of the sale without advice. A buyer and lender may want the legal position reviewed.

Known damage, blocked drainage or an access obstruction should be addressed through the appropriate professional route. Do not carry out work on shared land or another owner's property without the necessary agreement.

## Tell your solicitor about disagreements early

If there has been a disagreement about parking, access, boundaries, maintenance or use, give your solicitor the relevant correspondence and facts. They can advise what must be disclosed and how enquiries should be answered.

Avoid arguing about the issue during a viewing or minimising a documented concern. A clear account handled through the legal process is better than a buyer learning about it from someone else.

Do not contact a neighbour with a new legal proposal solely because the property is going on the market without first taking advice. A rushed change can create more uncertainty rather than less.

## Show access accurately in the marketing

Plan photographs and viewings so buyers can see the approach, turning space, gates and relationship with neighbouring homes. James can explain what is visibly shared while leaving the legal detail to the solicitors.

Keep vehicles positioned in a way that does not suggest exclusive use of shared ground. The floor plan should focus on the property, while the listing wording should avoid unverified boundary claims.

If access changes at busy times, tell James so viewings can be arranged sensibly. Practical honesty helps attract buyers who are comfortable with the real setting.
