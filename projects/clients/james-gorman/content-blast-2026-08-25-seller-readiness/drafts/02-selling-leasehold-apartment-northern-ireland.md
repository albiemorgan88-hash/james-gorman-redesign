# Selling a Leasehold Apartment in Northern Ireland

Selling an apartment usually involves information about both the home and the wider building. Buyers and their solicitors may need to understand the lease, service charges, management arrangements, insurance, common areas and any planned work.

Starting early matters because some of those records may come from a management company, managing agent, landlord or another third party rather than from the seller alone.

## Give the lease to your solicitor first

Send your solicitor the lease, title information and any later variations or agreements as soon as you decide to sell. They can confirm the legal description, the remaining lease position, rights over common areas and the restrictions that may affect a transfer.

Do not rely on memory for ground rent, parking rights, storage spaces, pets, letting or alterations. The written documents and current management information are what the legal process will examine.

Land and Property Services maintains the Land Registry, Registry of Deeds and Statutory Charges Register. The [nidirect guide to property and land information](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/how-find-property-and-land-information) explains what those records cover.

## Gather service-charge and management information

Collect recent service-charge statements, payment records, budgets, building insurance information and correspondence about maintenance or planned work. Include contact details for the management company or managing agent and tell your solicitor about any amount that is disputed or outstanding.

Buyers may want to understand what the charge covers and whether significant work has been discussed. Provide the genuine documents rather than estimating future costs or saying a project will not happen.

Ask your solicitor what formal information must be requested and who needs to supply it. If a fee or consent is required, knowing that early can prevent the request from being left until a buyer is waiting.

## Check parking, storage and common areas

An apartment listing should distinguish between a space included in the title, an allocated space, visitor parking and informal use. The same applies to sheds, roof spaces, gardens, bin areas and storage cupboards.

Give your estate agent the accurate position before photographs and viewings. If a feature is shared or subject to rules, describe it that way. A buyer should not discover late in the process that a space shown with the apartment is not legally included.

Tell your solicitor about any access, maintenance or neighbour issue that has affected your use of the property. They can advise what information needs to be given during the transaction.

## Organise records for work inside the apartment

Keep approvals, invoices, warranties and certificates for alterations, windows, heating, electrics, plumbing or structural work. Some changes may also have required consent under the lease or from the building's management arrangements.

Do not assume that work inside the front door was automatically the owner's decision alone. Ask your solicitor to check the lease and any approvals if a wall, window, service or common part was affected.

If information is missing, identify the gap honestly. It is better to investigate before marketing than to make a claim that cannot be supported when the buyer's solicitor raises enquiries.

## Present the apartment and the building clearly

Buyers will judge the internal condition, but they will also notice the entrance, corridors, lift, gardens, parking and general upkeep. Tell your agent about access arrangements for viewings and any security process for the building.

Make the apartment easy to understand. Show the usable layout, natural light, storage and outlook without overstating room uses that are not supported by the floor plan or approvals.

If you need an EPC, the Department of Finance says it must be obtained before a building is marketed for sale. Its [Northern Ireland EPC guidance](https://www.finance-ni.gov.uk/articles/energy-performance-certificates) explains the seller's responsibility.
