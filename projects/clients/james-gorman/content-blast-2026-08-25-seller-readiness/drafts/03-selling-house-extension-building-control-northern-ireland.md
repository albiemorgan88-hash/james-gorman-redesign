# Selling a House with an Extension in Northern Ireland

An extension can improve the space and appeal of a home, but buyers will usually want the paperwork to match what they can see. Planning permission and Building Regulations are separate processes, and the documents needed depend on the work and the property.

The best time to check the file is before the house is photographed, not after a buyer's solicitor or surveyor asks the first question.

## List exactly what changed

Write down when the extension was completed, who designed and built it, and what rooms or services were created. Include later alterations to roofs, doors, windows, drainage, heating and electrics.

Gather drawings, quotations, invoices, warranties, professional appointments and completion records. If the work happened before you owned the house, give your solicitor everything you received when you bought it.

Do not describe an informal conversion as an approved bedroom, annexe or separate dwelling unless the records support that use. Accurate room descriptions protect trust and help James prepare the marketing properly.

## Treat planning and Building Regulations separately

Planning permission considers the development and use of land, while Building Regulations deal with standards for construction. An extension may need one, both or neither depending on its details, but a seller should not make that judgement from memory.

The [nidirect planning guidance](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/planning-permission-when-apply) explains when planning permission may be required. The separate [Building Regulations approval guide](https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/how-apply-building-regulations-approval) explains the approval routes for building work.

Contact the relevant council teams or an appropriate professional if the file is unclear. Ask your solicitor how the available records affect the sale and what information should be given to a buyer.

## Check completion evidence and guarantees

Look for approval decisions, inspection records and completion evidence rather than assuming an initial application proves the finished work was signed off. Keep structural calculations, drainage information and certificates for electrical, heating or glazing work where they apply.

Read warranties before saying they transfer. Some require notice, a fee or particular maintenance, and some cover only part of the work. Give the actual document to your solicitor and let the buyer's advisers assess it.

If records are missing, do not invent replacements or approach the problem with a last-minute promise. Your solicitor can explain the available options for the specific transaction.

## Make sure the condition supports the extra space

Inspect the extension for known leaks, cracking, damp, ventilation problems and unfinished work. Use the appropriate surveyor or contractor where a concern needs diagnosis.

Fresh decoration can improve presentation, but it should not be used to conceal an unresolved defect. A buyer's survey may focus on the junction with the original building, roof, drainage and structural changes.

Show the room dimensions and purpose accurately. Buyers value usable space, natural light, heating and a layout that works with the rest of the home, not floor area alone.

## Prepare for buyer and lender questions

Give your estate agent and solicitor the property file early. A buyer may need enough information for their surveyor, solicitor and mortgage lender to assess the extension.

Clear records do not guarantee that every buyer will respond in the same way, but they make the position easier to explain. Missing information may lead to further enquiries, specialist advice or a longer decision.
