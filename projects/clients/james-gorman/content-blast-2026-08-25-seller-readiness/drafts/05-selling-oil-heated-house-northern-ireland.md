# Selling an Oil-Heated House in Northern Ireland

Oil heating is familiar across many Northern Ireland homes, but a buyer may still want clear information about the boiler, tank, controls, service history and running arrangements.

Good records make the system easier to understand. They do not guarantee a sale or remove the need for a buyer's own survey and specialist advice.

## Identify the boiler and controls

Record the boiler make, model and approximate installation date if the paperwork confirms it. Gather manuals, invoices, warranties and service records, including information about replacement burners, pumps or controls.

Do not describe a system as recently serviced unless you have the dated record. If the boiler is not working properly or there is a known fault, use a suitably qualified technician and keep the resulting report or invoice.

Show buyers how the heating is controlled without promising a particular running cost. Household routines, insulation, weather, fuel prices and thermostat settings all affect consumption.

Before a viewing, check that the timer and room controls are easy to identify. Leave technical adjustments to the appropriate professional, but make sure James knows where the controls are and which records can be shown to an interested buyer.

## Check the oil tank and visible pipework

Make sure the tank can be seen safely and that the access route is clear. Note its material, approximate age and capacity only where the documents or markings support that information.

If you notice damage, movement, staining, a strong oil smell or a suspected leak, stop and seek appropriate professional advice. Do not attempt to repair a tank or fuel line yourself.

Gather records for tank replacement, bases, barriers and pipework. A buyer's surveyor or technician may consider the tank's condition and location, so avoid hiding it behind stored items or vegetation.

## Be clear about the fuel that remains

Tell your solicitor how the remaining oil will be handled at completion and follow their advice. Do not assume that a rough tank reading should simply be added to the property price or settled informally with the buyer.

Keep recent delivery records if they help identify the supplier and system use, but treat them as household history rather than a promise of future consumption.

If access is needed for tanker deliveries, show the route accurately. Tell James about gates, shared access or timing restrictions that a buyer should understand during a viewing.

## Prepare the EPC information

An Energy Performance Certificate is required before a property is marketed for sale in Northern Ireland. The assessor considers the building and services, including the heating system, using an approved assessment method.

The Department of Finance explains the process and seller responsibilities in its [Northern Ireland EPC guidance](https://www.finance-ni.gov.uk/articles/energy-performance-certificates). Give the assessor accurate information and let the certificate report the rating.

Do not promise that replacing one part of the heating system will create a particular rating or sale-price uplift. Ask the appropriate assessor or installer about proposed improvements.

## Deal with repairs before presentation claims

A clean, working boiler area and an accessible tank are easier to present than a system surrounded by clutter. That does not mean cosmetic work should hide leaks, corrosion or unresolved faults.

If you replace equipment before selling, keep the commissioning, warranty and payment records. Consider the years of use your household expects as well as the possible buyer response before committing to a major project solely for sale.

Tell your estate agent and solicitor about known issues. Buyers can then arrange the inspection or advice they need without relying on an unsupported reassurance.
