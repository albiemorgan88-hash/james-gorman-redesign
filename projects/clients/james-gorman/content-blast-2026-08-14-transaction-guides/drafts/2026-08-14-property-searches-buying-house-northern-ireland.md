# What Searches Are Done When Buying a House in Northern Ireland?

When you buy a house in Northern Ireland, your solicitor investigates the legal title and arranges the searches and enquiries needed for that property and transaction. The exact set is not identical for every purchase. It depends on the title, location, property type, lender and anything revealed by the papers.

Searches do not replace a survey or mortgage valuation. They deal with legal and official-record questions, while a survey considers the physical property.

## What does the solicitor check first?

The seller's solicitor prepares the contract and title papers. The buyer's solicitor reviews who owns the property, what land is included, rights that benefit or burden it, mortgages or charges and any restrictions that may affect the buyer.

The solicitor also raises pre-contract enquiries. These can cover boundaries, access, services, alterations, planning, Building Control, guarantees, fixtures and other matters relevant to the transaction.

If something is missing or unclear, the solicitor may ask for further documents, an explanation, insurance or another appropriate solution. The buyer should wait for their own solicitor's advice before treating a point as resolved.

## Which official searches may be involved?

The work may include searches of the Land Registry or Registry of Deeds, depending on how the property title is registered. Bankruptcy, statutory charges, planning or other public-record searches may also be relevant.

Your solicitor decides what is required and explains the results. A list found online cannot determine the correct searches for a particular Derry house, apartment, rural property or new build.

The Law Society of Northern Ireland consumer guide explains that the buyer's solicitor investigates title, checks searches and considers replies to enquiries before advising on the contract.

## Do searches show the physical condition of the house?

No. Searches and legal enquiries may reveal approvals, notices or title matters, but they do not inspect the roof, structure, damp, wiring, heating or drainage condition.

A lender's valuation is also limited and is carried out for the lender. Buyers should consider an appropriate survey and any specialist inspections recommended for the property.

## What if a search reveals a problem?

Ask your solicitor what the finding means for ownership, use, lending and resale. The response may be a document or clarification from the seller, further investigation, a change to the contract, insurance, negotiation or a decision not to proceed.

Do not rely on a verbal assurance from a viewing or assume that every issue is equally serious. The legal effect and the practical risk can differ.

If the point also affects condition or value, your solicitor may suggest speaking to the surveyor, lender or estate agent. Each professional has a different role.

## How can buyers keep the process moving?

Instruct a solicitor as soon as the offer is accepted, provide identity and source-of-funds information through the firm's verified process and answer questions promptly. Tell the solicitor about your intended use of the property, mortgage, gifted deposit or any deadline that could affect the purchase.

Read documents carefully and ask when you do not understand a term. Searches are useful because they create questions before you become contractually committed, not because they can promise a risk-free home.
